# 1)  ABSTRACT

With a growing focus on ESG and sustainable investing, a report from August 2021 re-

vealed that more than 70% of ESG funds fail to align with the global climate targets set in

the Paris agreement from 2015. To further research this concerning matter, this paper

conducts a comprehensive analysis of Danish sustainable investment funds and their en-

vironmental level of sustainability. By utilizing ESG ratings as well as sectorial weights, we

develop a novel scoring method attempting to address the environmental impact of com-

panies relative to global climate targets. Using this innovative approach to portfolio analy-

sis, we evaluate the funds based on their underlying holdings and the allocation of capital.

This study furthermore examines the potential of improving the environmental impact and

attach importance to abandoning the current view on sustainability in the investment

## funds.

The results indicate a suboptimal capital allocation of the Danish investment funds in re-

lation to global climate targets. Sectors with limited capacity to drive environmental

change constitute the majority of capital, namely investments into Technology, Healthcare

and Financials. Meanwhile, sectors with the largest capacity for environmental impact

have an insignificant allocation, which highlights the need for a greater focus on these

sectors in the portfolio selection process. This sectorial focus does however come at the

expense of expected Sharpe Ratio but given the current suboptimal risk-diversification of

the Danish funds, the results of this study indicate that major improvements regarding

environmental impact could be achieved without compromising financial performance.

The results further show that despite slightly improved ESG ratings in sustainable funds

compared to traditional market indices, little to no enhancement in environmental impact

is observed. This indicates a lack of transparency in both sustainable investments and

ESG ratings, which calls for more standardization as well as regulation.

# 2)  INTRODUCTION

The world is standing in the middle of a global environmental crisis, and encouragements

to act are being made from policymakers, companies, activists, and the general popula-

tion. However, despite the general support for stopping climate change, this is not re-

flected in the actual results of climate change. The latest climate change report from the

IPCC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) has showed that climate impacts are

more widespread and disastrous than previously expected. The forecasts made in the re-

port indicate that the consequences of climate change will only escalate exponentially in

severity from the current temperature rise of 1.1°C to 2°C (IPCC, 2022). Further, the Paris

Agreement (2015) heavily emphasize the importance of limiting the temperature increase

to a “well below 2 degrees” threshold, preferably to 1.5 degree Celsius, compared to pre-

industrial levels (United Nations, 2015). These reports call to action, as we will need to act

now, and not tomorrow. The solutions are out there, but we need to support and change

## our ways drastically.

To help solve the climate crisis, the EU has in 2020 put the EU Green Deal in motion, which

will, if succeeded, turn the EU carbon neutral by 2050 (EU, 2022 a). A crucial part of this

plan is the funding of the green transition – with a funding target of approximately € 1

trillion. While the biggest part of this funding will come from the EU and governmental

budgets, more than a quarter should be provided by private companies (Harvey & Rankin,

## 2020).

It is therefore naturally paramount that incentives are made for private companies to un-

dertake risky green projects in order to deliver on their part of the global responsibility. It

is therefore interesting to investigate the power of the open market, and especially the

effect of the stock market on company incentives. While stock prices and performance-

based compensation often leads to myopic behavior from management of listed compa-

nies, it is crucial that the biggest corporations start to adapt a longer time horizon and

prioritize a green transition. Over the past decade the level of ESG and socially responsible

investing has risen exponentially (Widyawati, 2019). Despite the increased focus on ESG,

the largest proportion of institutional investors mainly incorporate ESG in order to improve

their Sharpe Ratio, and to a lesser extent due to moral or ethical considerations (Amel-

Zadeh & Serafeim, 2018). A report further showed that based on research of 723 ESG-

# 2.1) RESEARCH QUESTION

To ask managers of sustainable investment funds to simply abandon a financial perfor-

mance target and adopt a philanthropic investment method is not realistic and would con-

flict with their financial fiduciary duty towards their clients. But actions on climate change

are long overdue, and the investments will need to live up to their sustainable label. While

completely abandoning their risk-adjusted return is not appropriate, managers will need

to skew their portfolio towards companies that contribute significantly to the green transi-

tion. However, as we do not have sufficient regulation or transparent ESG reporting in

place, the label of being a sustainable investment fund tells little detail on the actual com-

## position or degree of sustainability in a fund.

We will in this paper undertake an analysis of the sustainable investment products offered

to private investors on the Danish market. We will unpack the funds and by using financial

theory together with both financial and ESG data we will evaluate their financial efficiency

as well as their alignment with global climate targets. To do this, our primary focus will be

# 2.2) RESEARCH PERSPECTIVE

This thesis builds upon positivistic theory of science, and further uses the critical rational-

## ism theory commended by Popper (Popper, 1973).

In general, the classical positivism is a ramification of the theological theory, tracing all the

way back to 1.000 BC, later developing into a metaphysical approach, before at last reach-

ing the positivistic stage of development by the start of 1850. At this stage, humans face

the observable, and the aim of science is to reach verification when conducting theory. In

other words, the objects of science must exist no matter the observer.

The classical positivism is later replaced by the logical one, driven by empirical observa-

tions and experiments. The logical positivism uses clearly stated assumptions, applying

mathematical methods as a best practice to sort-out these empirical observations. The

methodology of positivism is quantitative, in the sense that the objective is only achievable

if it is based upon investigation of observable data, meaning that anything that cannot be

perceived or be observed, cannot be a subject of investigation (Brier, 2017). In general,

the logical positivism trusts the principal of verification, letting the investigator determine

the truth of a theory through empirical testing (Misak, 1995).

# 3)  LITTERATURE REVIEW

# 3.1) ESG ALPHA

Multiple authors have demonstrated how integration of ESG in the portfolio selection pro-

cess can add an alpha (difference between expected and required return according to the

security market line), when compared to a non-restricted index such as the MSCI World

Index and similar indices. In this section we will try to highlight some of the papers that

states that ESG investing can add an alpha, but also a paper arguing the opposite.

## 3.1.1) TILT AND MOMENTUM STRATEGIES

The paper ‘Can ESG add Alpha? An Analysis of ESG Tilt and Momentum Strategies’ argues

that institutional investors can yield a positive alpha by adopting the ESG approach in two

investment strategies: The tilt and the momentum strategy (Nagy, Kassam, & Lee, 2016).

The ESG Tilt strategy assumes that ESG scores are linked to future performance of the

stock, meaning that this strategy includes assets with good ESG ratings (Using MSCI ESG

Ratings), while also minimizing the active risk of the portfolio. The authors use an 8-year

period of historical risk and return data to decompose the active risk and return of the ESG

tilt strategy. Over the period, the tilt strategy outperformed the MSCI World Index by 1.06

percentage point, mainly driven by style factors (value, size etc.) and stock-specific contri-

bution. When diving deeper into the strategy, Nagy et al. (2016) discovers that the ESG tilt

strategy leads to a bias in favor of mid-cap stocks and stocks with lower idiosyncratic vol-

## atility, and a tilt away from value stocks.

The ESG Momentum strategy does not look at the specific ESG score, but the increase in

ESG score over the past 12 months, in other words, companies that have improved their

ESG ratings significantly over the last year. This strategy also outperformed the MSCI World

Index over the 8-year period, even more than the ESG Tilt Strategy, where the strategy

yielded an active excess return of 2.23 percentage points, compared to the MSCI World

Index. The significant positive contribution factors within this strategy were style factors

and stock-specific return, just like with the tilt strategy.

The paper highlights that in both strategies, the stock-specific source was the main reason

for the outperformance, which could indirectly be attributed to the ESG signals. Nagy et al.

(2016) also concludes that both strategies saw a tilt towards mid-cap stocks since the end

## 3.1.2) FACTOR MODELS AND SECTOR BIASES

On the other hand, some papers argue that ESG strategies do not yield a positive alpha,

but the alpha gained on such strategies origins from other sources such as sector over-

weight and style factors. Bruno, Esakia and Goltz (2022) construct 6 different ESG strate-

gies, and test them in two different asset universes, to see if such a strategy is capable of

yielding a positive return. The strategies are: 1) Overall ESG-rating, 2) overall E-rating, 3)

overall S-rating, 4) overall G-rating, 5) ESG momentum and 6) a combination of ESG-score

and ESG-momentum. The two asset universes are 1) US Universe and 2) Developed mar-

ket outside of the US (Bruno, Esakia, & Goltz, 2022). When using simple risk and return

metrics, Bruno et al. (2022) concludes the same as previous papers, that almost all the

six ESG-strategies in the two universes yield an additional annual return and a positive

CAPM alpha. However, when adjusting for performance factor exposure, the result be-

## comes more blurred.

To nuance the results, Bruno et al. (2022) both uses Fama-French 6-factor model (FF-6),

in their regression, but also tests the strategies using Asness-Frazzini 5-factor model (AF-

5) and the Q 5 Factor Model by Hou et al. (Q-5). When doing so, the authors do not find a

single significant positive result on any of the six strategies in the two different asset uni-

verses, which suggests no evidence of positive alpha for such ESG strategies. This implies

that investors cannot improve their Sharpe Ratio by using ESG investment strategies. The

tests also suggest that the ESG strategies heavily load on Equity Style Factors such as

market, size, value, momentum etc. Further, Bruno et al. (2022) also accounts for sector

## 3.1.3) ESG INTEGRATION PARADOX

As shown by multiple papers and authors, ESG can in fact lead to a better risk-adjusted

return and better long-term value creation. However, the investor must be fully committed

to the ESG integration, as a not-wholehearted implementation can lead to a poorer finan-

cial performance. Cappucci (2018) illustrates this by “The Gold Standards”, where the in-

vestment performance will decrease when ESG intensifies, until a certain point, where the

financial performance will increase with increasing ESG intensity.

But if investors can yield a better risk-adjusted return with full ESG integration, why is the

strategy not more frequently adopted? Cappucci (2018) tries to illustrate this ESG integra-

tion paradox. He explains the paradox as the fact that a majority of respondents answers

that they care for ESG when making investment analysis/decisions, but when asked how

it is done, 72% says that they do not know much about how much they should spent on

independent verification of ESG reporting, which means that the integration of ESG often

becomes discretionary and unsystematic (Cappucci, 2018). In addition, a substantial per-

centage of investors believe that full integration of ESG is the strategy with the greatest

positive performance, but the same investors also state that those strategies are not the

## best fit for their firm.

The paper indicates how only 1.7% of all investment strategies receive the highest ESG

score (ESG 1), whereas nearly 86% of the strategies ends in the two lowest ESG-categories

(ESG 3 and ESG 4). This clearly shows that there is a great distinction between what is be-

lieved to be the best investment strategy, and what strategies are actually adopted.

Cappucci (2018) suggests four possible reasons for the lack of ESG integration in invest-

ment decisions. The greatest barrier to more ESG integration is the lack of standards for

# 3.2) EU REGULATIONS

As mentioned above the lack of standards, and the disagreement about how to categorize

and determine ESG is one of the greatest barriers to integration of ESG into investment

decisions (Cappucci, 2018). As we will uncover in more depth in 3.5) ESG data is not as

reliant and transparent as needed (Berg, Koelbel, & Rigobon, 2022). With a wide range of

independent ESG rating agencies failing to agree on a uniform assessment method, the

output of ESG integration might differ greatly depending on what supplier’s input is used.

For stock listed companies several different voluntary standards exist as well, some more

detailed and demanding than others. A company interested in reporting on their sustaina-

bility related issues can choose between numerous voluntary frameworks, such as GRI,

Integrated Reporting, SASB, etc. (GRI, 2022) (Integrated Reporting, 2022) (SASB, 2022).

It is great that frameworks for standardized sustainability reporting is being developed, but

it does, however, create the same issues seen in ESG data ratings. With different stand-

ards having different methodologies, it can be difficult to compare the output from differ-

# 3.4) ESG INVESTMENT METHODS

Within ESG investing, one might experience different approaches towards the subject. The

“classical” one is using ESG scores which can be used from a variety of sources like Bloom-

can be useful tools to pick out the investment strategy with the lowest imprint on the en-

gation and adaptation scores to assess the companies that perform well environmentally.

Mitigation investment is all investment activities that are positively related to reducing

Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and increases exposure to low carbon economy. This

could be improvement of energy efficiency through new technologies and renewable ener-

gies to name some. The adaptation investment refers to investments that improve the

resilience of an investment portfolio to the physical impact of climate change. This could

for example be protection or immunization of agriculture, food supply and infrastructure

Bender et al. (2019) uses six scores to construct four mitigation objectives and uses two

adaption scores to construct one adaption objective. The mitigation scores are Carbon-

company’s GHG Reduction Plans. The objective for the adaptation scores is to maximize

exposure to climate adaptation ratings. The approach is then to use a mean-variance op-

timization where the objective function balances exposure across all five objectives.

# 3.6) BUSINESS SUSTAINABILITY

The previous section highlighted the difficulties and the inadequacy of the current imple-

mentation of ESG into the investment selection and reporting process. As ESG is both a

very novel concept (Doyle, 2018) and far scoping, it naturally leads to less consensus com-

## pared to a P/L statement.

This lack of consensus has been described by Dyllick & Muff (2016) in their article ‘Clari-

fying the meaning of sustainable business’ (Dyllick & Muff, 2016). The authors highlight

that there seems to be different views and paradigms on the term business sustainability

and thereby the scope of ESG. More specifically they divide business sustainability into

three paradigms. The first paradigm is seen where the company chooses to go away from

the purely financial focus where shareholder value is seen as the only motive of a firm. In

this first paradigm the shareholder value view is refined in the way that the firm acknowl-

edges that different stakeholders such as NGOs, media and governments have interest in

the activities of the firm. The firm thereby incorporates sustainability issues into their de-

cision making. The focus is however still on shareholder value, as the sustainability issues

are incorporated as part of potential risk areas. The firm thereby acknowledges that busi-

ness as usual will in the future lead to decreased profits since stakeholders will affect the

## business environment.

The second paradigm of business sustainability takes a large leap away from shareholder

value and integrates the triple bottom line. The firm will in this paradigm measure success

on three parameters – economic, ecological, and social (similar to ESG). The idea of the

triple bottom line is that profits or success is measured on three different but equally val-

uable dimensions. The firm will therefore no longer only integrate sustainability matters to

increase or maintain profits, but furthermore to create a positive impact on all three levels.

The final paradigm of business sustainability is what the authors determine as true busi-

ness sustainability. The difference from the previous paradigm is that the company no

longer acts with an organizational perspective of inside-out but rather an outside-in per-

spective. The change of perspective is what changes the whole dynamics of a company.

Instead of setting the company at the center of all activities, true business sustainability

starts with the most pressing sustainability challenges. The company asks themselves how

## 4.1) MARKOWITZ AND TOBINS PORTFOLIO THEORY

Markowitz (1952) introduced the analysis of mean-variance optimization in his framework

“Portfolio Theory” (Markowitz, 1952). The framework and his later contribution to the field

won him a Nobel prize in 1990 and is acknowledged and highly relevant to this day. The

theory allows investors to find the portfolio with the highest expected return for a given

level of volatility/variance. By doing so, Markowitz constructed an efficient frontier, thereby

giving the investor the opportunity to optimize his/her portfolio given the investors optimal

## trade-off between risk and return.

The theory explains that the covariance within an investor’s portfolio determines its incre-

mental risk, meaning that the risk of an investment cannot be evaluated isolated (Berk &

De Marzo, 2017). The general idea is that investors consider different portfolios for a given

future period, and always want to maximize return while minimizing risk. The theory was

further developed by James Tobin by introducing and incorporating the risk-free rate into

the model (Tobin, 1958). Markowitz explained that an investor might choose any place on

the efficient frontier, but Tobin (1958) considered the implications of allowing investors to

combine risky securities with a risk-free investment (Berk & De Marzo, 2017). Including the

risk-free rate, one can construct the tangency portfolio to the efficient frontier, which is the

portfolio on the efficient frontier with the highest Sharpe ratio. The Sharpe ratio is given by

## 4.2) CAPITAL ASSET PRICING MODEL

In extension to the portfolio theory addressed above, the Capital Asset Pricing Model

(CAPM) allows us to identify the efficient portfolio of risky assets without having any

knowledge of the expected return of each security. The model was derived by Treynor

(1961), Sharpe (1964), Litner (1965), and Mossin (1966) (Munk, 2019).

The CAPM instead uses the optimal choices investors will make given the total market

portfolio (market portfolio = all stocks and securities in the market). The CAPM model relies

on three assumptions (Berk & De Marzo, 2017): 1) Investors buy and sell securities at

competitive market prices and can borrow at the risk-free rate. 2) Investors hold a portfolio

that maximizes their expected return for the desired level of volatility, meaning that the

portfolio is always on the efficient frontier. In other words, maximizes the Sharpe Ratio. 3)

Investors have identical expectations for volatilities, correlation and expected returns of

## securities.

at market prices, then all investors will identify and demand the exact same portfolio of

securities. And as every security is owned by someone, then the sum of all portfolios must

equal the market portfolio. And as all investors demand the same portfolio with the highest

## 4.4) THE ESG EFFICIENT FRONTIER

This framework by Pedersen, Fitzgibbons, and Pomorski (2021) proposes a theory that

incorporates ESG into the investor’s portfolio selection process, where the objective is to

maximize utility for a given level of ESG preferences. They develop on the classical capital

asset pricing model (CAPM) by adding ESG, thereby constructing an ESG-adjusted CAPM-

model to determine the equilibrium asset prices (Pedersen, Fitzgibbons, & Pomorski,

## 2021).

Pedersen et al. (2021) start by constructing three different investor types: 1) Type-U (ESG-

unaware investor), who seeks to maximize his mean-variance utility regardless of the un-

derlying ESG score of the portfolio. 2) Type-A (ESG-aware investor), who has mean-variance

preferences, but uses the ESG scores to get an updated view on risk and expected return.

3) Type-M (ESG-motivated investor), who gets utility from a portfolio with high-scoring ESG

companies. Pedersen et al. (2021) illustrates how the different investor types use the

trade-off between ESG and Sharpe Ratio by constructing the ESG-efficient frontier:

## 5.1) INTRODUCTION TO ESG SCORES

Environmental, Social, and Governance, in short ‘ESG’, are factors used to evaluate com-

panies (and countries) on their overall sustainability. It consists of three metrics and is

often used in the investment process (Robeco, 2022). As mentioned in the literature re-

view, ESG scores and ratings from ESG rating agencies are not in any way perfect. They

are, however, currently the best tool available for asset managers if they are to use a rela-

tively unbiased data input. The bias in ESG scores comes from the different methodologies

used by ESG rating agencies. As the literature shows, ESG scores are not fully transparent

and are not converging across rating agencies. Asset managers will therefore have an un-

willing bias in relation to their data providers underlying methodology. Given these imper-

fections, ESG scores still provide value to asset managers, and the ratings are still funda-

## mental in the investment decision.

bn.) a severity rate of 0.67 and small cap (<$2 bn.) a severity rate of 1. This implies that

small companies will be more severely punished, as scandals in larger companies are

more likely to be discovered by newspapers and other medias.

# 6.2.5) HOLDING PERIOD

The investor’s holding period must be considered when determining the expected market

return. ETF investing is a classic passive investment method, letting the buyer own a small

portion of a complete portfolio, thereby lowering the cost for the investor, compared to an

actively managed fund. The general idea behind passive investment is to ride out short-

term volatility, meaning that the holding period usually is medium- to long-term. Anadu,

Kruttli, Mc Cabe, Osambela and Shin (2018) finds that investors of passive funds, such as

ETFs, has a smaller incentive to chase performance, possibly due to usage of passive

funds as a long-term investment such as for retirement and similar long-term objectives.

To define what an appropriate holding period for an ETF investment is, we consider one

business cycle as the best estimation. Alvarez-Ramirez, Rodriguez, and Ibarra-Valdez

(2020) investigate the oscillation of business cycles using the Dow Jones Index and finds

that the dominant period of cycle is approximately 10.5 years for returns and 8.0 years for

trading volume. The estimation shows coherence with the Juglar cycle, an estimation of a

fixed investment business cycle, which usually lays in the range of 7 to 11 years. Using

these estimations, we reach an optimal holding period of 10 years.

# 6.2.7) BETA

The beta is another important input when computing the expected return, as the beta will

describe the systematic risk that investors take on by holding a portfolio or single stocks.

Beta is not to be mistaken for volatility, as the two terms describe different measurements.

Volatility describes the total risk, thereby both market (systematic) risk and the firm-spe-

cific risk, whereas beta only accounts for the systematic part of risk (Berk & De Marzo,

2017). If a stock has a beta of 1.5, it will imply that the investment carries 50% more

systematic than an investment in the market portfolio (or other investments with a beta of

## 1).

The computation of beta can be done in different ways, depending on the return length of

the data used. Typically, beta is computed using monthly, weekly, or daily return data. Ob-

viously, daily return will need a much more comprehensive dataset, which is what we have

used for our paper, thereby being as accurate in our computations as possible. Whether a

using different return intervals. Hawawini (1983) examines this issue by computing beta

for different return intervals, both daily, weekly, biweekly, triweekly, and monthly, showcas-

ing a great deviation in results (Hawawini, 1983). By constructing a so-called “q-ratio” us-

ing correlation between individual stocks and the S&P 500 index, both on same business

days and for lagged values, Hawawini (1983) were able to find coherence between this q-

ratio and the change in beta when altering the length of the return interval. The q-ratio is

inversely related to a security’s market capitalization, implying that companies with a small

q-ratio will have a large market cap., and vice versa for large q-ratios. Further, he finds that

the beta of securities with a larger q-ratio (small market cap.) than the market average will

have a decreasing beta when the return interval is shortened, and vice versa when the q-

ratio is below the market average (large market cap.). This implies that companies with a

large market cap., relative to the market average, will have an increasing beta when the

return interval is shortened, the opposite being present for securities with a small market

cap. compared to the market average (Hawawini, 1983).

Evidently, this does not tell us which beta is more correct, but instead how the beta-coef-

for our computation we are likely to face larger beta-coefficient for larger companies, com-

pared to the output if a 10-year monthly beta was used. On the other hand, smaller com-

## 6.7) RELIABILITY, VALIDITY, AND SUFFICIENCY

To ensure that our analysis builds upon desirable qualities of the underlying measurement,

we will have to be certain that our quality assurance is as good as possible. To guarantee

this, we will have to check to basic properties of empirical measurement, namely reliability

and validity (Carmines & Zeller, 1979). Further, we will have to be certain that our analysis

flow into a conclusion containing a sufficient level of coherence with the data foundation

(Olsen & Pedersen, 2003). These three elements will be explained and investigated below.

# 6.7.1) RELIABILITY

Reliability covers quality of the data used, in the sense that it should be as less defective

as possible and try to reflect reality to the highest degree (Olsen & Pedersen, 2003). Our

approach has followed a rather reliable approach for collecting price data. The price data

is retrieved using an API supplied by our data provider, Refinitiv (Refinitiv, 2022 e). As price

data should not differ, no matter the provider, we should be provided with correct price

data using Refinitiv. This also implies that the methodology should be completely replica-

ble if repeated in the future for the same time span, even with another data provider -

Unless the API, for some reason, should fail to interpret certain data cells correctly. To

come around this issue, we manually went through the data set, identifying potential out-

liers and misinterpretations, and have removed or corrected any flaw in the data, to elimi-

nate potential errors, see 6.1.2). However, as we are not able to go through nearly 9 million

data cells, to see if every single one of them is correctly retrieved, we have performed spot

tests and mathematical computation to check if the data was as flawless as possible.

Regarding ESG data, the retrieval of the data was done using the Excel API as well. This

implies that the retrieved Refinitiv ESGC scores, ESG pillar score and environmental cate-

gory scores should be correctly interpreted. However, as argued in 3.5) ESG Data Reliance,

ESG data is not capable of reflecting the true image of the actual sustainable performance

of assets, first, as ESG data ratings are only correlated to a certain extent. This implies that

the scores of different data providers vary a great deal, meaning that if we have used

Bloomberg, MSCI or another data provider, the outcome of the analysis may differ, as each

of these providers uses different interpretation methods to quantify ESG performance.

Thus, we are aware that the ESG scores used in this task do not fully represent a correct

image of reality of companies’ true sustainability.

Second, as ESG data obviously is not as transparent as price data, it is rather difficult to

get the full understanding of how the scores are conducted, as multiple metrics for each

score are used. In our analysis, we will use our own conducted Environmental Delta Score

(EDS) as the measure of ‘true environmental sustainability’. Yet, as explained in 5.3), the

methodology of computing the ESG scores as well as the EDS relies upon the performance

of the given company compared to the peer group. For that reason, an oil producing com-

impact. Yet, the reliability of the data is challenged, but as Refinitiv provides the best and

most advanced methodology for conducting ESG data, Refinitiv is, in our opinion, the data

provider, that delivers data with the best possibility of mirroring reality.

# 6.7.2) VALIDITY

Validity covers the coherence between the subject we want to investigate, and the data

set used. As sustainability is not a very well-defined term, we will have to construct a proxy

with underlying constraints to determine the best estimation for true sustainability, namely

the Environmental Delta Score. Thus, the data used in the paper does not correspond per-

fectly with what we want to research, as no data provider are able to deliver sufficient

measurement for companies’ level of engagement of reaching the climate goals proposed

in the Paris Agreement and in the climate change report from the IPCC. Because of this,

we felt compelled to construct the EDS proxy to shed light on companies that offer true

sustainability. And as Refinitiv’s ESG scores and their materiality matrix help us build this

proxy, the advantage of picking Refinitiv as data provider is manyfold, as they both provide

rather good and transparent data as well as insight in their computation, that allow us to

further develop on the data provided. This creates a rather sufficient level of validity to

answer our research question, that we would not have been able to attain with other data

providers. We appeal that the launch of the EU Taxonomy will yield even more validity in

# 6.7.3) SUFFICIENCY

Sufficiency is the last element of the quality assurance of our paper. Sufficiency covers the

fact that we cannot conclude something, that the data foundation does not allow us to

conclude (Olsen & Pedersen, 2003). Researchers have a tendency of overestimating the

implications of own data, which can yield criticism and skepticism towards the subject

investigated. To come around this issue, our approach will be rather prudent, not equating

ESG scores with the level of sustainability. Instead, by composing meticulously theoretical

arguments and proxies as quantification of companies’ willingness to contribute to the

green transition, we are, to some extent, able to generalize upon the subject of true envi-

ronmental sustainability. Thus, we acknowledge that the data used for this study is not

sufficient to speak the actual truth of environmental sustainability but are satisfactory to

### 8.2) THE TRADEOFF BETWEEN SUSTAINABILITY AND SHARPE RATIO

# 8.2.1) EDS VS. SHARPE RATIO

The literature regarding ESG alpha shows multiple contradicting indications of whether

integration of ESG and sustainability will provide investors with an additional risk-adjusted

return compared to traditional indices such as MSCI World Index, see 3.1). Our paper does

not seek to investigate past performance but instead highlights the impact on expected

financial performance by integrating sustainability deeper in the investment decision.

Building on the theoretic paper on ESG-integration by Pedersen et al. (2021), our findings

show that increasing the environmental focus towards global climate targets comes at a

tradeoff, where the investor must sacrifice expected Sharpe Ratio (SR). Whether this sac-

rifice of SR is realized ex-post is outside the scope of this paper but given that the markets

are efficient any alpha realized, positive or negative, will be due to idiosyncratic risk. Our

results furthermore located the expected cause of the reduced SR, as an increasingly am-

bitious EDS-integration caused Basic Materials and Utilities companies to be weighted

more heavily. The reduced SR is therefore presumably caused by an overexposure to these

## sectors.

The difference between the expected ex-ante SR and the realized ex-post SR touches on

another noteworthy insight given by our analysis. As shown in Figure 29, both the Danish

sustainable funds as well as the global equity funds by i Shares report SR far below the

efficient EDS frontier. When comparing the funds with our constructed portfolio, having an

EDS of 30 (similar to the level of the examined funds), the technology-bias of the global

equity funds becomes very visible. The examined funds were all heavily allocated in the

technology sector (average 30% allocation), whereas our model only reported 14.8 % allo-

cation. As previously mentioned, the technology-bias is mainly due to large capital alloca-

Whereas our model indicates that this allocation is suboptimal seen from a risk-diversifi-

stocks will have provided enormous alpha, as they all have average annualized returns

above 15% for the past 10 years (Solberg & Lauricella, 2021).

However, our best tool for predicting future financial performance is still the expected SR

based on historic data. Our analysis indicates that current investment funds, sustainable

as well as traditional, are far below optimal EDS-levels. It therefore entails that even though

EDS comes at a tradeoff for Sharpe ratio, improvements in EDS could be achieved in the

funds without compromising Sharpe Ratio. This ties back to the extreme exposure to the

## technology sector and especially US tech giant.

## 8.3.3) IMPORTANCE OF THE EU-TAXONOMY AND CSDR

As described in the previous section, ESG ratings currently have several shortcomings that

questions the validity of relying on them in the investment decision. The report by ACCF

highlighted possible solutions to this problem, mainly focused on more standardization in

reporting and more regulation from policy makers to make non-financial reporting manda-

tory (Doyle, 2018). Without these structures in place, ESG ratings will naturally have a

lacking validity as rating agencies will not have sufficient reliable data inputs to provide

## non-biased ratings.

A possible solution to these challenges can be found in the upcoming legislation being

implemented in the EU. As described in 3.2), the EU-taxonomy has already been published

along with the first two Delegated Acts concerning climate change mitigation and climate

change adaptation. The taxonomy addresses the problem of standardization in reporting

by clearly defining a binary classification system to economic activities. This would enable

companies to designate the relevant parts of their revenue as taxonomy-aligned or as

green revenue. While a binary classification system has shortcoming of reporting the mag-

nitude of a certain taxonomy-aligned activity, it will still be a great improvement to the

current state of transparency. The taxonomy alone would only contribute partially to solving

the problem, as it would, without any regulation, most likely just end up as another volun-

tary reporting framework such as GRI or Integrated Reporting. The possible solution to the

problem of ESG data transparency therefore comes in combination with the CSRD.

The report from ACCF underlined the importance of both regulation of reporting on non-

financial information and the role of auditing these reports. The CSRD addresses both

these areas as it would require all publicly listed companies to report according to the EU

sustainability reporting standards, and furthermore require auditing of the reports. While

clearly addressing the most present transparency issues in ESG-reporting, the CSRD has

one limitation given that the scope naturally only applies to companies in member states

of the EU. It could however be anticipated that a successful implementation of both the

# 9)  CONCLUSION

Our paper starts by addressing the issue of ESG rating transparency, and how ESG data at

its current level is not sufficient to yield a true and fair impression of the level of environ-

mental impact in a macroeconomic context. To get around this issue we construct a proxy

of true sustainability, named ‘Environmental Delta Score’, that greatly illustrates the true

environmentally improving capability of a company. Further, we construct weighted sector

scores allowing us to deeply investigate the composition and true sustainability of specific

Danish investment funds, as well as similar international investment funds.

Our findings suggest that these Danish sustainable investment funds do not target the

most critical industry groups, instead allocating capital to industries that show limited ca-

pacity to drive environmental change on a global level. This inconsistency is most promi-

nent in the Technology sector where the Danish funds have a substantial bias towards the

US technology giants, which causes a suboptimal risk-diversification seen from a finan-

cially theoretical point of view. Our analysis indicates that the Danish sustainable funds do

not live up to their environmental responsibility related to global climate targets and could

drastically improve their environmental alignment with global climate targets, still holding

both expected financial performance and other ESG dimensions equal to the current per-

## formance levels.

The analysis further suggests that the explanation for the disproportion in the Danish

funds, is due to their heavy allocation of capital within the sectors of Technology,

Healthcare, and Financials. In order for the asset managers of the sustainable funds to

improve their environmental impact, their portfolio composition should be less exposed to

such sectors that insufficiently contributes to the green transition, and conversely invest a

larger allocation in sectors targeting environmentally critical subjects such as Basic Mate-

## rials and Utilities.

In addition, the analysis highlights that the Danish sustainable funds do not significantly

differentiate from traditional investing, proxied by the i Shares World and i Shares ACWI

ETFs. By using our proprietary score, the Environmental Delta Score, we show that the

improved Environmental Scores of the Danish sustainable funds, compared to the market,

were mainly driven by improved Environmental Scores in lesser important industries. This

implies that even though the funds have better Environmental Scores, their capability of

yielding a positive climate contribution on a macroeconomic level is rather limited due to

## their sector exposure.

Further, the analysis touches upon the different investment approach applied in thematic

sustainable funds, namely the i Shares Global Clean Energy and Rize Sustainable Future

of Food. Both funds showed vastly improved sector distributions in relation to environmen-

tal impact, as their sector exposure is very narrow. Our findings further showed that inves-

tors, to some extent, must forego potential Sharpe Ratio when investing in such funds. The

allocation of capital in fewer sectors naturally decreases the expected Sharpe Ratio, as

the portfolio will be skewed towards these climate targeting sectors, and thereby lowering

## the risk-diversification.

The very same is present for our optimized models. By constraining our model to equal the

level of social, governance, and controversy scores we illustrated how an ideal portfolio

would look like when optimizing for the Environmental Delta Score. Our findings suggest a

heavy exposure to Basic Materials and Utilities, which are the main drivers to the exceed-

ingly high Environmental Delta Score. We further conduct an Environmental Delta Score

efficient frontier, in short, the EDS Efficient Frontier, by opposingly maximizing for Sharpe

Ratio while constraining the model for different levels of Environmental Delta Score. This

frontier clearly illustrated how investors must forego expected Sharpe Ratio when improv-

## ing the Environmental Delta Score.

Our analysis has been conducted using ESG ratings provided by Refinitiv. For the integra-

tion of environmental characteristics in the investment decision, ESG ratings have several

shortcomings. Ratings are dependent on the peer group or sector, making companies from

different sectors difficult to compare. Our paper has tried to address this by utilizing sector

weights as a method for proxying capacity to drive environmental change. However, ESG

ratings do still have transparency issues as well as several structural biases, such as a

bias towards large-cap companies. Given the current state of ESG ratings, our findings can

therefore not stand alone, and further research must be done within this field using more

reliable data. More specifically we have identified the EU taxonomy as a potential alterna-

tive to ESG ratings, which could address the challenge of standardization. The upcoming

EU directive, CSDR, would promote the use of the EU taxonomy and would further require

more auditing of company reports. With these upcoming structural changes, we encourage

future researchers to conduct our analysis with more reliable and valid data in order to

provide more transparency in the space of sustainable investing.
